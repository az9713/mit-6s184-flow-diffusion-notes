1.1 A generator must reproduce a distribution 04:37

A generator produces a collection of possible outputs, not one preferred answer. Suppose a sensor simulator must generate scalar measurements with mean two and variance four. Returning two every time gets the mean right and eliminates all variability. Returning a standard normal draw preserves randomness but gives the wrong location and scale. A useful simulator must reproduce the distribution specified by the task. This small problem will let us check exactly what a flow model changes, where randomness enters, and how a numerical solver can spoil a correct construction.

Let $d$ be a positive integer and let $x\in\mathbb R^d$ represent an output. A color image with height $H$ and width $W$ has $d=3HW$ scalar entries after flattening. This representation records pixel values; it does not itself explain visual similarity or the meaning of a prompt. Let $P_{\mathrm{data}}$ denote the target probability law, a rule assigning probabilities to measurable sets of outputs. A random vector $X$ has this law when $\Pr(X\in A)=P_{\mathrm{data}}(A)$ for every such set $A$. We abbreviate this statement as $X\sim P_{\mathrm{data}}$.

When the law has a density with respect to ordinary volume, write that density as $p_{\mathrm{data}}:\mathbb R^d\to[0,\infty)$. It satisfies

$$P_{\mathrm{data}}(A)=\int_A p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)\,dx,\qquad \int_{\mathbb R^d}p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)\,dx=1.$$

The density at a point is not the probability of generating that exact point. A singleton has zero volume, so a law with a density assigns it probability zero. Densities also change when coordinates are rescaled. Consequently, identifying high density with perceptual quality is a modeling motivation, not a coordinate-independent theorem about human preferences. Matching a distribution is a precise mathematical goal; choosing the distribution and evaluating perceptual quality remain separate problems.

A dataset is a finite collection of observed outputs, written $x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(M)}$, where $M$ is the number of examples. The superscript indexes examples rather than time. The usual statistical idealization treats them as independent samples from $P_{\mathrm{data}}$. Their empirical law assigns mass $1/M$ to each example, counting repeated examples separately. This empirical law is discrete even when the population law has a density. Reproducing only those atoms amounts to memorization; learning a population model requires statistical assumptions beyond the existence of a sampler.

High-dimensional Gaussians illustrate another reason to distinguish density from representative samples. Let $G_1,\ldots,G_d$ be independent standard normal variables and write $G=(G_1,\ldots,G_d)$. The density is greatest at the origin, but the squared radius $R=\|G\|^2$ has mean $d$ and variance $2d$. Each squared coordinate has mean one and variance two; independence adds these quantities. For any $\epsilon\gt0$, applying the second-moment bound to $R-d$ gives $\Pr(|R/d-1|\ge\epsilon)\le2/(d\epsilon^2)$. Most mass therefore lies near squared radius $d$ when $d$ is large. Selecting only the maximum-density point would miss that mass. This calculation concerns Gaussian geometry; it supplies no perceptual metric for images.

Conditional generation specifies an additional observed variable $Y$, such as a class label or text prompt. At an allowed value $y$, the goal is sampling from $P_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot\mid Y=y)$. Correct unconditional proportions do not ensure correct conditional outputs. Suppose $Y$ requests a cat or a dog with equal probability. A model that ignores $Y$ and independently chooses either output class with probability one half has the correct unconditional class proportions. It nevertheless satisfies the requested class only with probability one half: the two successful joint events, cat with cat and dog with dog, each have probability one quarter. Conditional training must learn the relationship between the output and $Y$, in addition to the output's marginal law. We first analyze a fixed target law.

Running case. The notation $\mathcal N(m,v)$ denotes a one-dimensional normal law with mean $m\in\mathbb R$ and variance $v\gt0$. We begin with $X_0\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ and seek $X_1\sim\mathcal N(2,4)$. The subscript is generation time, with $t\in[0,1]$ increasing from simple noise to target data. State and time are dimensionless in this example. A transformation $G(x)=2+2x$ already solves it, because $\mathbb E[2+2X_0]=2$ and $\operatorname{Var}(2+2X_0)=4$. The question is how a differential equation can implement such a transformation.

1.2 A vector field specifies velocities, and a flow specifies destinations 13:02

A trajectory is a time-indexed state $t\mapsto x_t\in\mathbb R^d$. A velocity field is a function $u:[0,1]\times\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d$, written $u_t(x)$. Its value specifies both direction and speed at state $x$ and time $t$. The field does not return the final sample. An ordinary differential equation, or ODE, asks for a differentiable trajectory satisfying

$$\frac{dx_t}{dt}=u_t(x_t),\qquad x_0=a,$$

where $a\in\mathbb R^d$ is the prescribed initial state. If the state carried physical units, $u_t$ would have state units per unit time. An additional initial velocity is unnecessary for this first-order equation: the equation fixes it as $u_0(a)$. A second-order physical equation can be represented as a first-order equation only after adding velocity to the state.

When the solution is unique, its destination at time $t$ defines the flow map $\psi_t(a)=x_t$. The identities $\psi_0(a)=a$ and $\partial_t\psi_t(a)=u_t(\psi_t(a))$ hold for every admissible initial state. A trajectory follows one starting point. A flow collects these trajectories for all starting points. For a time-dependent field, evolution from an intermediate time requires a two-time map; one should not assume the one-time maps obey the autonomous semigroup relation $\psi_{t+s}=\psi_t\circ\psi_s$.

The lecture's linear contraction gives a direct check. For a constant $\kappa\gt0$, take $u_t(x)=-\kappa x$. The proposed solution $x_t=e^{-\kappa t}a$ starts at $a$, and differentiation gives $-\kappa e^{-\kappa t}a=-\kappa x_t$. Every state approaches zero, although it reaches zero at finite time only when it began there. This last distinction matters: an invertible finite-time transformation can contract a distribution without collapsing it to an atom.

For our generation task, contraction alone moves in the wrong direction. Set $\lambda=\log 2$, where $\log$ is the natural logarithm, and choose the time-independent field $u_t(x)=\lambda(x+2)$. Introduce the shifted scalar state $z_t=x_t+2$. Then $dz_t/dt=\lambda z_t$ with $z_0=a+2$, so

$$\psi_t(a)=e^{\lambda t}(a+2)-2=2^t(a+2)-2.$$

At $t=1$, the destination is $\psi_1(a)=2a+2$, exactly the affine transformation needed above. The field itself is not this map: it returns $\lambda(x+2)$ at the current state, while integrating the field accumulates those velocities. Confusing these objects would replace a correct endpoint transformation by one small local update.

We can now make the starting point random. Define $X_t=\psi_t(X_0)$ while keeping the field fixed. Conditional on one draw of $X_0$, the complete trajectory is deterministic. Across initial draws, it is random. The ODE therefore needs no fresh noise during integration to generate a nondegenerate distribution. A neural ODE replaces the analytic field by $u_t^\theta$, where $\theta$ is a finite collection of learned parameters. Those parameters stay fixed while a sample is generated. This derivative-parameterization viewpoint is the construction introduced in Chen et al., Neural Ordinary Differential Equations, Section 1; it does not by itself specify how to learn a useful field.

1.3 Existence and uniqueness require hypotheses 22:48

A numerical program needs an unambiguous mathematical process to approximate. Continuity of the field alone does not guarantee a unique trajectory. On the half-line $x\ge0$, consider $dx_t/dt=2\sqrt{x_t}$ with $x_0=0$. For any waiting time $c\in[0,1]$, the function that is zero up to $c$ and equals $(t-c)^2$ afterward satisfies the equation. At the joining point its derivative is zero from both sides. A rule choosing one of these solutions would be extra information not contained in the stated ODE.

The missing control is local sensitivity to state. Use the Euclidean norm $\|\cdot\|$. A field is uniformly globally Lipschitz in state if some finite constant $L\ge0$ satisfies $\|u_t(x)-u_t(y)\|\le L\|x-y\|$ for every $x,y\in\mathbb R^d$ and $t\in[0,1]$. This controls differences of velocities, not their absolute magnitudes. The square-root example fails the condition near zero because $2\sqrt{x}/x=2/\sqrt{x}$ has no finite upper bound as positive $x$ approaches zero.

Proposition 1.1 — a sufficient global ODE condition. Suppose $u$ is continuous in time and state, uniformly globally Lipschitz in state with constant $L$, and $C=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|u_t(0)\|$ is finite. Then each initial state has one solution throughout $[0,1]$. Two solutions beginning at $a,b$ satisfy $\|\psi_t(a)-\psi_t(b)\|\le e^{Lt}\|a-b\|$.

Proof. On an interval of length $\delta$ with $L\delta\lt1$, the integral operator $T(f)(t)=a+\int_0^t u_s(f(s))\,ds$ contracts continuous paths in the supremum norm: $\|T(f)-T(g)\|_\infty\le L\delta\|f-g\|_\infty$. Repeated application converges to a unique fixed point, which solves the integral equation and hence the ODE. The same construction starts at later times. To rule out escape to infinity during extension, note that $\|u_t(x)\|\le C+L\|x\|$. Its integral inequality gives $\|x_t\|\le(\|a\|+Ct)e^{Lt}$, a finite bound on our interval.

For the sensitivity statement, let $D(t)=\|\psi_t(a)-\psi_t(b)\|$. Subtracting the integral equations gives $D(t)\le\|a-b\|+L\int_0^tD(s)\,ds$. Define $F(t)=\|a-b\|+L\int_0^tD(s)\,ds$. Then $D\le F$ and $F'\le LF$, so differentiation of $e^{-Lt}F(t)$ shows it is nonincreasing. Therefore $D(t)\le e^{Lt}\|a-b\|$. Setting $a=b$ proves uniqueness. ∎

Global existence is stronger than local existence. A smooth field can still explode: $dx_t/dt=x_t^2$, $x_0=1$, has solution $x_t=1/(1-t)$ before time one. Direct differentiation verifies the equation, and the solution diverges at the endpoint. Its derivative with respect to state is $2x$, so the global Lipschitz bound fails. Conversely, global Lipschitz continuity need not imply differentiability everywhere. A finite feedforward composition of affine maps and Lipschitz activations is globally Lipschitz: each layer has a finite Lipschitz constant, and composition multiplies those constants. An activation can still have a corner, so the resulting field need not be differentiable everywhere.

These examples refine the sufficient conditions in the course notes, Theorem 3, PDF p. 8. Continuously differentiable fields with uniformly bounded state Jacobians satisfy a Lipschitz bound by integrating the Jacobian along a line segment. A differentiable flow and a differentiable inverse require additional differentiability hypotheses; uniqueness alone should not be called a proof of differentiability. For the scalar affine running case, all these properties are explicit: $\psi_t^{-1}(x)=2^{-t}(x+2)-2$, and its derivative is $2^{-t}\gt0$.

Uniqueness also controls whether trajectories can merge. Fix a terminal time $t$ and destination $b$. Reverse elapsed time by writing $y_r=x_{t-r}$ for $r\in[0,t]$. The reversed path solves $dy_r/dr=-u_{t-r}(y_r)$ with $y_0=b$. This reversed field retains the same global Lipschitz bound, so Proposition 1.1 gives a unique backward path. Two initial states that reached $b$ at time $t$ would yield two such paths, contradicting uniqueness. The finite-time map is therefore one-to-one, and solving backward from any $b$ gives its inverse on this global setting.

The conclusion restricts what regular finite-time flows can represent exactly. In one dimension a continuously differentiable invertible map with nonzero derivative transports a density to another density by change of variables. It cannot turn a Gaussian into a finite collection of point masses while retaining these hypotheses. A neural generator may approximate narrow clusters or operate with a smoothed data distribution, but an exact empirical law and a regular continuous-density target are different mathematical objects. The distinction matters when one specifies what an ideal training objective is supposed to achieve.

1.4 Numerical integration introduces a second model 28:30

Choose a positive integer $N$, a uniform step size $h=1/N$, and grid times $t_n=nh$. Write $\widehat X_n$ for the computed state at $t_n$, distinguishing it from the exact $X_{t_n}$. Explicit Euler freezes the current velocity over one step:

$$\widehat X_{n+1}=\widehat X_n+h\,u_{t_n}(\widehat X_n),\qquad n=0,\ldots,N-1.$$

This update replaces $u_s(X_s)$ over one interval by its left-endpoint value. Fix an initial state and suppose its exact trajectory is twice differentiable with $\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|\ddot X_t\|\le M_2$ for a finite constant $M_2$. Taylor's theorem bounds the one-step defect by $M_2h^2/2$. Define the state error $E_n=\|\widehat X_n-X_{t_n}\|$ and initialize the numerical and exact paths equally, so $E_0=0$. Subtract the numerical update from the exact step and use the state Lipschitz constant $L$. The resulting recurrence is $E_{n+1}\le(1+Lh)E_n+M_2h^2/2$. For $L\gt0$, summing the geometric recurrence gives

$$E_N\le\frac{M_2h}{2L}\bigl((1+Lh)^N-1\bigr)\le\frac{M_2h}{2L}(e^L-1),\qquad Nh=1.$$

For $L=0$, summing the recurrence instead gives $E_N\le M_2h/2$. The terminal error is therefore bounded by a constant times $h$ under the stated hypotheses. This is a bound for the chosen initial state. An expected-error bound for random initialization additionally needs integrable control of the constants. The result does not guarantee monotone error reduction for arbitrary rough or unstable problems.

The running case exposes the distribution error without Monte Carlo noise. Each Euler step satisfies $\widehat X_{n+1}+2=(1+\lambda h)(\widehat X_n+2)$. Define $r_N=(1+\lambda/N)^N$. Repeating the recurrence gives $\widehat X_N=r_N(X_0+2)-2$. Because an affine transformation preserves normality,

$$\widehat X_N\sim\mathcal N\bigl(2(r_N-1),r_N^2\bigr),\qquad X_1\sim\mathcal N(2,4).$$

The finite-step program samples the first law, even though its underlying ODE has exactly the desired endpoint law. Expanding the logarithm gives $\log r_N=\lambda-\lambda^2/(2N)+O(N^{-2})$. Here $O(N^{-2})$ denotes a remainder bounded in magnitude by a constant times $N^{-2}$ for sufficiently large $N$. Thus $r_N=2[1-\lambda^2/(2N)+O(N^{-2})]$. The leading mean error is $2\lambda^2/N$, and it tends to zero at first order.

Stability imposes a different constraint. For the contractive test equation $dx_t/dt=-\kappa x_t$, Euler multiplies the state by $1-\kappa h$. Decay of every nonzero numerical initial state requires $|1-\kappa h|\lt1$, equivalently $0\lt\kappa h\lt2$. At the upper boundary the scheme alternates without decay. Above it, the numerical state grows although the exact solution decays. Accuracy concerns the size of the approximation error; stability concerns whether the recurrence amplifies disturbances. A solver must address both.

The following standalone check takes only a step count, computes the finite-step Gaussian moments, and prints deterministic output. It reads no files, uses no network, and trains no model. Its positive-integer requirement is enforced by the downloadable implementation. Analytic moment propagation isolates discretization error; random draws would add a second source of uncertainty.

import math

steps = 16
growth = (1 + math.log(2) / steps)**steps
mean = 2 * (growth - 1)
variance = growth**2
print(f"mean={mean:.6f}, variance={variance:.6f}")
assert 0 < mean < 2 and 1 < variance < 4
mean=1.942047, variance=3.884934

1.5 Sampling concerns the transported law 33:26

Let $P_t$ be the law of $X_t=\psi_t(X_0)$. For any measurable output set $A$, the event $X_t\in A$ is exactly the event $X_0\in\psi_t^{-1}(A)$. Therefore $P_t(A)=P_0(\psi_t^{-1}(A))$. This construction is called the pushforward of $P_0$ by $\psi_t$, written $P_t=(\psi_t)_\#P_0$. It describes probability transport without requiring a density. The symbol $\# $ labels this operation; it is not multiplication.

In our scalar example the map is increasing and has a differentiable inverse. Let $m_t=2(2^t-1)$ and $a_t=2^t$, the mean and standard deviation of $X_t$. A change of variables in an interval probability gives the density

$$p_t(x)=\frac{1}{a_t}\,p_0\!\left(\frac{x-m_t}{a_t}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,a_t}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(x-m_t)^2}{2a_t^2}\right].$$

The factor $1/a_t$ preserves total probability while the distribution widens. Substituting $z=(x-m_t)/a_t$ into its integral gives $\int p_t(x)\,dx=\int p_0(z)\,dz=1$. At time one, the peak moves to two and its height is halved, even though the target has been reached exactly. Peak density by itself would misleadingly suggest a decrease in quality. Figure 1.1 shows this mass-preserving change.

A flow sampler can simulate trajectories without explicitly evaluating $p_t$. Conversely, having a density formula does not automatically make sampling inexpensive. Continuous normalizing flows connect dynamics to density evolution when differentiability conditions hold; Chen et al., Section 4 develops that connection. Here the elementary affine change of variables is sufficient. Training by flow matching will use a different route that need not evaluate a likelihood for every generated sample.

There are at least three distinct questions: whether a target field exists, whether training identifies a suitable field, and whether a solver accurately integrates the identified field. Our analytic construction settles the first for one Gaussian pair and gives a benchmark for the third. It says nothing about learning from finite image data. Even exact agreement of mean and variance would be insufficient to establish agreement of arbitrary non-Gaussian laws. A distribution can have those moments while concentrating on two points.

A coupling makes the effect of numerical error on observables precise. Here a coupling means constructing two random outputs on the same probability space; use the same initial draw for $X_1$ and $\widehat X_N$. Let $f:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R$ be a function satisfying $|f(x)-f(y)|\le\|x-y\|$. Provided the expectations exist, the triangle inequality and this bound give $|\mathbb E[f(X_1)]-\mathbb E[f(\widehat X_N)]|\le\mathbb E[\|X_1-\widehat X_N\|]$. Thus a pathwise error bound controls discrepancies in every such observable. In the scalar running case, the observable $f(x)=x$ already detects the mean bias. Equality of a few chosen observables is much weaker than equality of distributions.

The same argument separates field error from solver error. Suppose an ideal field $u$ and an approximate field $\widetilde u$ satisfy $\sup_{t,x}\|u_t(x)-\widetilde u_t(x)\|\le\eta$, where $\eta\ge0$, and $u$ is Lipschitz with constant $L$. Couple their exact trajectories through the same starting point, writing them as $X_t$ and $\widetilde X_t$. Their difference satisfies $D(t)\le\eta t+L\int_0^tD(s)\,ds$. The integrating-factor argument from Proposition 1.1 gives $D(t)\le\eta(e^{Lt}-1)/L$ when $L\gt0$, and $D(t)\le\eta t$ when $L=0$. Add the separate discrepancy between $\widetilde X_1$ and its numerical approximation to bound the total error. This is a deterministic sensitivity result under a uniform field bound, not a theorem that a small average training loss supplies such a bound.

1.6 Brownian noise scales with the square root of time 38:57

A stochastic process is a family of random variables indexed by time, together with their joint law. Knowing a separate distribution at each time does not specify how values at different times are related. An ODE with random initialization is already a stochastic process, but fixing its initial draw fixes its trajectory. A diffusion adds randomness during evolution. That extra input is described by a Brownian motion $W_t\in\mathbb R^d$.

Standard Brownian motion. Assume $W_0=0$, continuous sample paths, independent increments over disjoint time intervals, and $W_t-W_s\sim\mathcal N(0,(t-s)I_d)$ whenever $0\le s\lt t\le1$. Here $I_d$ is the $d$-dimensional identity matrix, so the covariance in each coordinate is $t-s$ and distinct coordinates are independent. Brownian motion is also called a Wiener process. We take its existence as a mathematical input; constructing its probability space is outside this chapter.

At grid times, independent standard normal vectors $\varepsilon_n\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d)$ give exact Brownian increments $\Delta W_n=\sqrt h\,\varepsilon_n$. Their means vanish and their covariance is $hI_d$. Summing $N$ independent increments over unit time gives covariance $NhI_d=I_d$. Drawing with scale $h$ instead gives covariance $Nh^2I_d=I_d/N$, which disappears under refinement. This mistake changes the limiting process rather than merely reducing its accuracy. It is the first check to make when simulated diffusion becomes implausibly deterministic at small step sizes.

The linear variance law follows from consistency of independent increments. In one coordinate, suppose stationary increments have variance $v(h)$ over length $h$. Splitting an interval into two independent pieces forces $v(h+k)=v(h)+v(k)$. With continuity and the normalization $v(1)=1$, rational subdivisions give $v(h)=h$, and continuity extends it to real times. A proposed standard deviation proportional to $h$ would instead give $v(h)=h^2$, contradicting this additivity.

Brownian motion is continuous but cannot be treated as an ordinary smooth velocity. One consequence can be derived without a full path-regularity theorem. In one dimension, define $Q_N=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(\Delta W_n)^2$. Gaussian increments give $\mathbb E[Q_N]=Nh=1$. For a standard normal scalar $G$, integration by parts against its density gives $\mathbb E[G^4]=3\mathbb E[G^2]=3$. Thus $\operatorname{Var}((\Delta W_n)^2)=2h^2$ and independence yields

$$\mathbb E[(Q_N-1)^2]=\operatorname{Var}(Q_N)=2Nh^2=\frac{2}{N}\longrightarrow0.$$

This is convergence in mean square, meaning that the expected squared difference tends to zero. A continuously differentiable path with derivative bounded by $B$ would have $Q_N\le NB^2h^2=B^2/N\to0$. Brownian paths therefore have fundamentally different small-scale behavior. The calculation explains why squared noise increments survive in stochastic calculus. It does not by itself prove the stronger theorem that Brownian paths are almost surely nowhere differentiable. The construction and numerical interpretation here agree with Higham, Sections 2–3, printed pp. 526–531.

1.7 Define an SDE through its integrals 55:23

Let $b:[0,1]\times\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d$, written $b_t(x)$, denote the stochastic drift. Assume that $b$ satisfies the continuity, uniform global Lipschitz, and finite-origin-bound hypotheses of Proposition 1.1. Let $\sigma:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)$ be a deterministic continuous diffusion amplitude. It multiplies all Brownian coordinates equally; a state-dependent diffusion matrix is outside this chapter's scope. Reserve $u_t$ for probability-transport velocity. With nonzero diffusion, the drift $b_t$ need not equal that velocity. Take $X_0$ independently of the Brownian motion. The stochastic differential equation, or SDE,

$$dX_t=b_t(X_t)\,dt+\sigma_t\,dW_t$$

means the integral identity $X_t=X_0+\int_0^t b_s(X_s)\,ds+\int_0^t\sigma_s\,dW_s$. The ordinary integral accumulates drift. The second is an Itô stochastic integral. This formulation avoids treating $dW_t/dt$ as an ordinary function. For the deterministic integrands needed here, the stochastic integral has an accessible mean square construction.

For a step function taking values $c_j$ on intervals $[a_j,a_{j+1})$, define its integral as $\sum_jc_j(W_{a_{j+1}}-W_{a_j})$. Independent centered increments make its mean zero and, in one coordinate, its variance $\sum_j c_j^2(a_{j+1}-a_j)$. Consequently, the mean square distance between integrals of two step functions equals the ordinary integral of their squared difference. Approximate continuous $\sigma$ by step functions in that squared-integral norm; the corresponding random sums converge in mean square. This defines $M_t=\int_0^t\sigma_s\,dW_s$, with

$$\mathbb E[M_t]=0,\qquad \mathbb E[M_tM_t^{\mathsf T}]=\left(\int_0^t\sigma_s^2\,ds\right)I_d.$$

The superscript $\mathsf T$ denotes matrix transpose. The process has a continuous version, which we invoke as a foundational result. For deterministic integrands, the integral is Gaussian: its defining sums are Gaussian, and their means and variances converge. General random integrands require additional measurability conditions and an Itô construction. Those are not needed for the additive-noise examples here. Higham's integral formulation and Euler–Maruyama derivation, Section 4 provide the numerical connection.

The integral variance rule exposes a limitation of informal small-step notation. Take zero drift, $X_0=0$, and the continuous coefficient $\sigma_t=t^{1/4}$. Freezing the coefficient at time zero predicts a zero increment over $[0,h]$. The actual stochastic integral has variance $\int_0^h s^{1/2}\,ds=(2/3)h^{3/2}$. Its root mean square size is $\sqrt{2/3}\,h^{3/4}$, which is larger than order $h$ as $h$ tends to zero. Continuity therefore does not justify a frozen-noise remainder that is $h$ times a random quantity tending to zero in mean square. The integral definition remains valid; the numerical approximation must be analyzed under its actual regularity assumptions.

For uniqueness, fix one continuous realization of $M_t$ and write $V_t=X_t-M_t$. The integral equation becomes $V_t=X_0+\int_0^t b_s(V_s+M_s)\,ds$. For that fixed path, this is an ordinary integral equation. Its field is continuous and retains the state Lipschitz constant of $b$. Its value at $V=0$ is bounded because $M$ is bounded on the compact time interval and $b$ has at most linear growth. Proposition 1.1 therefore gives one $V$ and hence one $X$. Picard iterates at time $t$ use only the initial data and noise through $t$, so the limit does too.

This is a strong, pathwise construction: on a probability space with specified independent $X_0$ and Brownian motion, it produces a solution without extra random choices. Two solutions driven by the same noise and initial value coincide because subtracting their equations cancels the stochastic integral and leaves the Lipschitz inequality from Proposition 1.1. Uniqueness in law instead concerns the distribution of entire trajectories for specified input laws. Equality of separate time marginals alone is weaker and does not determine that trajectory law.

Euler–Maruyama freezes the drift and diffusion integrands at the left endpoint, giving

$$\widehat X_{n+1}=\widehat X_n+h\,b_{t_n}(\widehat X_n)+\sigma_{t_n}\sqrt h\,\varepsilon_n.$$

The $\varepsilon_n$ are independent across steps and independent of $X_0$. A new draw is required each step; reusing one draw correlates increments. With $\sigma\equiv0$ and $b=u$, this recovers the Euler flow scheme. Adding nonzero noise while retaining $b=u$ generally changes the transported law. The relation between a chosen probability path, its transport velocity, the stochastic drift, and the diffusion amplitude must be derived before claiming equivalent samplers.

1.8 The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck test separates three errors 1:09:23

Return to the target $\mathcal N(2,4)$. A mean-reverting process might seem an alternative way to reach it. The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck, or OU, process has constants $\kappa\gt0$, center $\mu\in\mathbb R$, and noise amplitude $\sigma\ge0$:

$$dX_t=\kappa(\mu-X_t)\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t.$$

The centered process $X_t-\mu$ contracts in its drift, while noise continually replenishes spread. Set $J_t=e^{\kappa t}(X_t-\mu)$. A deterministic differentiable factor obeys the ordinary product rule against this additive-noise process. To justify the absence of a quadratic correction, let $f$ be that deterministic factor. On a partition, the noise cross terms are $\sigma\sum_j\Delta f_j\Delta W_j$. Independence and $|\Delta f_j|\le\|f'\|_\infty\Delta t_j$ bound their mean square by $\sigma^2\|f'\|_\infty^2\sum_j(\Delta t_j)^3$, which tends to zero with the maximum interval length. Cross terms with the integrated drift vanish because it has finite variation and the largest $|\Delta f_j|$ tends to zero. The product rule therefore gives the cancellation

$$dJ_t=\kappa e^{\kappa t}(X_t-\mu)\,dt+e^{\kappa t}\bigl[-\kappa(X_t-\mu)\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t\bigr]=\sigma e^{\kappa t}\,dW_t.$$

Integrate from zero to $t$, use $J_0=X_0-\mu$, and multiply by $e^{-\kappa t}$. The result is

$$X_t=\mu+e^{-\kappa t}(X_0-\mu)+\sigma\int_0^te^{-\kappa(t-s)}\,dW_s.$$

If $X_0\sim\mathcal N(m_0,v_0)$ independently of the Brownian motion, the integral and initial term are independent Gaussians. Here $m_0$ and $v_0$ are the initial mean and variance. The variance rule for deterministic stochastic integrals then yields

$$\mathbb E[X_t]=\mu+e^{-\kappa t}(m_0-\mu),\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_t)=e^{-2\kappa t}v_0+\frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}(1-e^{-2\kappa t}).$$

For $\sigma\gt0$, letting time grow without bound gives the Gaussian limiting law with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2/(2\kappa)$. This statement uses the same OU dynamics extended beyond the unit generation interval. Starting in that Gaussian law makes the moments and Gaussian law unchanged at every time; it is an invariant law. Starting elsewhere generally reaches it only asymptotically. An invariant distribution is not a promise about a finite terminal time.

Set $\kappa=1$, $\mu=2$, $\sigma=\sqrt8$, and $X_0\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$. The invariant law is our target, but at generation time one the mean is $2(1-e^{-1})=1.264241$ and the variance is $4-3e^{-2}=3.593994$. Thus even an exact OU solver would miss the target. This is a finite-horizon modeling mismatch. It exists before numerical error or learning error enters.

For Euler–Maruyama, write $q=1-\kappa h$. Its mean obeys $m_{n+1}-\mu=q(m_n-\mu)$, and independence of new noise gives $v_{n+1}=q^2v_n+\sigma^2h$. Summing the geometric series, when $q^2\ne1$, gives

$$m_N=\mu+q^N(m_0-\mu),\qquad v_N=q^{2N}v_0+\sigma^2h\,\frac{1-q^{2N}}{1-q^2}.$$

With four steps, the example gives mean $1.367188$ and variance $4.213882$. These differ from both the exact finite-time law and the target. Furthermore, if the discrete scheme is stable, its own long-time variance solves $v_*=q^2v_*+\sigma^2h$, hence $v_*=\sigma^2/(2\kappa-\kappa^2h)$. At $h=1/4$ this is $4.571429$, rather than four. Refining a stable simulation removes this discretization bias, but cannot remove the exact process's finite-time mismatch.

The deterministic program ch01.py checks these Gaussian laws through exact recurrences and saves its results in ch01-results.json. It also verifies that scaling noise by $h$ would make unit-time Brownian variance equal $1/N$. These are finite analytic implementation checks. They do not establish neural-model quality, perceptual diversity, or training convergence.

There is also a distinction between marginal and trajectory laws. A stationary OU process with $\mu=0$, $\kappa=1$, $\sigma=\sqrt2$, and $X_0\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ has $X_t\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ at every time. So does the static ODE $dX_t=0$ with the same initialization. Yet the ODE has $X_t=X_0$, whereas the OU process has covariance $\mathbb E[X_0X_t]=e^{-t}$. Equal one-time laws do not imply equal dependence across times. For endpoint generation, matching the endpoint law is the goal; for trajectory prediction or path-dependent quantities, the distinction is essential.

1.9 What the construction establishes

A flow or diffusion model separates the initial randomness, the dynamics, and the numerical solver. The Gaussian case provides an exactly solvable benchmark for each part. It proves that deterministic transport can generate a nondegenerate target law, exposes an ODE solver's endpoint bias, and shows why diffusion noise must scale as the square root of a step. The OU calculation then distinguishes invariant-law reasoning from finite-time generation.

The construction does not explain how to obtain a field from data. That is the central question of flow matching in Chapter 2. Nor does an arbitrary choice of diffusion coefficient preserve a flow's target distribution. Score functions and the associated probability-evolution equations will supply the missing relation. Here Brownian existence and the continuous version of deterministic stochastic integrals are invoked as foundational results; the elementary moment and uniqueness arguments are derived explicitly.

A useful implementation practice follows already: before training a large model, make a solver reproduce an exactly known distribution, test the noise scaling under refinement, and compare exact continuous-time laws with exact discrete recurrences. A sample plot can hide a mean shift or stationary-variance bias that these checks reveal immediately.

1.10 Problems with worked solutions

Problem 1.1 — moments do not identify the law

A generator returns zero or four, each with probability one half. Calculate its mean and variance. Does it solve the running Gaussian task? Give a measurable event distinguishing its law from $\mathcal N(2,4)$.

Solution

The mean is $(0+4)/2=2$. The squared deviation from two is four for either output, so the variance is four. Nonetheless, the event $A=\{0,4\}$ has probability one under this generator and probability zero under a law with a density. Matching the two moments is sufficient to identify a Gaussian law only after Gaussianity has been independently established. In the analytic checks above, Gaussianity follows from affine transformations of independent Gaussian variables.

Problem 1.2 — construct the flow between two scalar Gaussians

Let $X_0\sim\mathcal N(m_0,a_0^2)$, and prescribe differentiable functions $m_t$ and $a_t\gt0$ with their indicated initial values. Construct a velocity field whose trajectory is $X_t=m_t+(a_t/a_0)(X_0-m_0)$. Verify the initial condition, ODE, and endpoint law.

Solution

Differentiate the proposed trajectory: $\dot X_t=\dot m_t+(\dot a_t/a_0)(X_0-m_0)$. Since $X_t-m_t=(a_t/a_0)(X_0-m_0)$ and $a_t\gt0$, substitute $X_0-m_0=a_0(X_t-m_t)/a_t$. The velocity is $u_t(x)=\dot m_t+(\dot a_t/a_t)(x-m_t)$. At time zero, the proposed trajectory equals $X_0$. Its derivative is the stated velocity, so it solves the ODE. Its mean is $m_t$ and its variance is $(a_t/a_0)^2a_0^2=a_t^2$; affine Gaussianity proves $X_1\sim\mathcal N(m_1,a_1^2)$. Positivity of $a_t$ prevents the inverse and the velocity formula from becoming singular.

Problem 1.3 — an exact flow can have an inexact Euler sampler

Derive the Euler endpoint law for $u_t(x)=\log(2)(x+2)$ and $X_0\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$. Explain why increasing the number of independent generated samples cannot repair its mean error at a fixed step count.

Solution

Each update gives $\widehat X_{n+1}+2=(1+\log(2)/N)(\widehat X_n+2)$. Iteration gives $\widehat X_N=r_NX_0+2r_N-2$ with $r_N=(1+\log(2)/N)^N$. Thus its law is $\mathcal N(2r_N-2,r_N^2)$. At $N=16$, the computed mean is $1.942047$, below two. More independent samples make their sample mean concentrate around $1.942047$; they do not change that underlying mean. Refinement or a more accurate solver addresses discretization bias. Additional draws address sampling uncertainty.

Problem 1.4 — independent increments and roughness

Over unit time, use $N$ independent $\mathcal N(0,1)$ variables $\varepsilon_n$. Calculate the endpoint variance for increments $\sqrt h\varepsilon_n$ and $h\varepsilon_n$. For the correct increments, derive the mean and variance of $Q_N=\sum_n(\Delta W_n)^2$.

Solution

With $h=1/N$, independence gives endpoint variance $\sum_n h=N h=1$ under the square-root scaling and $\sum_nh^2=Nh^2=1/N$ under the incorrect scaling. For a correct increment, $\mathbb E[(\Delta W_n)^2]=h$ and $\mathbb E[(\Delta W_n)^4]=3h^2$. Its squared-increment variance is $3h^2-h^2=2h^2$. Hence $\mathbb E[Q_N]=1$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Q_N)=2Nh^2=2/N$. This proves mean square convergence of $Q_N$ to one. Reusing one normal draw for every increment would break independence: the endpoint would be $N\sqrt h\varepsilon$, with variance $N$, rather than one.

Problem 1.5 — an invariant distribution is not a finite-time guarantee

For $dX_t=(2-X_t)dt+\sqrt8\,dW_t$ with $X_0\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, derive the exact endpoint moments and the Euler–Maruyama four-step moments. Find the discrete stationary variance. Which errors survive if the step size tends to zero while the terminal time stays one?

Solution

The exact formulas give $m_1=2(1-e^{-1})=1.264241$ and $v_1=e^{-2}+4(1-e^{-2})=3.593994$. For four steps, $h=1/4$ and $q=3/4$. Therefore $m_4=2[1-(3/4)^4]=1.3671875$. The variance separates into the decayed initial variance and accumulated noise:

$$v_4=(3/4)^8+2\frac{1-(3/4)^8}{1-(3/4)^2}=4.2138824463.$$

The stationary recurrence solves $v_*=(3/4)^2v_*+2$, giving $v_*=32/7=4.5714285714$. Refinement converges toward the exact endpoint moments and eliminates the recurrence's discretization bias. The discrepancy between that exact endpoint law and $\mathcal N(2,4)$ remains; changing the dynamics or terminal horizon is needed to address it.

Problem 1.6 — identical marginals, different trajectories

Compare the static process $A_t=A_0$, $A_0\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, with the stationary process $dB_t=-B_tdt+\sqrt2\,dW_t$, $B_0\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ independently of $W$. Show that every time marginal agrees, but calculate the variance of the increment over a lag $r\gt0$ to distinguish the processes.

Solution

The static marginal is standard normal. For the OU process, the explicit solution gives mean zero and variance $e^{-2t}+1-e^{-2t}=1$, and it is Gaussian. Over a lag $r$, the transition formula is

$$B_{t+r}=e^{-r}B_t+\sqrt2\int_t^{t+r}e^{-(t+r-s)}\,dW_s.$$

The integral is independent of $B_t$, so $\operatorname{Cov}(B_t,B_{t+r})=e^{-r}$. Subtracting twice this covariance from the sum of the marginal variances gives

$$\operatorname{Var}(B_{t+r}-B_t)=1+1-2e^{-r}=2(1-e^{-r})\gt0.$$

The corresponding static increment is identically zero. One-time marginals therefore cannot determine the joint trajectory law.
