6. Discrete diffusion: learning the rates of a jump process

A sequence of tokens cannot follow the Gaussian trajectories of the preceding chapters. It can change one symbol, wait, and change another. The object we learn is therefore a matrix of instantaneous jump rates. The central learning mechanism survives: construct an easy conditional path, average its dynamics under a posterior, and learn that posterior from corrupted examples.

This chapter follows Lecture 5, the final recording of MIT 6.S184 (2026). Its running example is an original two-token language with just two valid sentences, 00 and 11, equally likely. Starting with two masks, can we generate the correct joint distribution? A model that gets each token right half the time can still generate an invalid sentence half the time. That distinction will test every stage of the construction. The mathematical spine is the course notes, §7, PDF pp54–65 and appendix C, pp74–76, together with the Lecture 5 slides; research extensions and original experiments are identified where used.

6.1 From moving vectors to jumping states [01:28]

Let $\mathcal V$ be a finite vocabulary and $L$ a positive integer giving sequence length. The state space is $\mathcal S=\mathcal V^L$. Its size is $|\mathcal V|^L$, even when the vocabulary and length are individually modest. A state $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_L)$ is a complete sequence. A random process $X_t\in\mathcal S$, with generation time $0\leq t\leq1$, starts from noise at time zero and approaches data at time one. Its probability mass function is the row vector $q_t$, whose entry $q_t(x)$ equals $\Pr(X_t=x)$.

A continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) has the Markov property: conditional on the present state and time, its future distribution does not require earlier states. Its sample trajectories are piecewise constant, with random jumps. This differs from the continuous sample paths of the Brownian models studied earlier. The deterministic evolution equation will describe probabilities of states, not a continuous motion of an individual token through intermediate token values. General stochastic equations can describe jump processes; the relevant replacement here is specifically a finite-state CTMC.

Definition: source-row jump generator. For distinct states $x,y$, $R_t(x,y)\geq0$ is the instantaneous rate of a jump from $x$ to $y$. Set the total exit rate $r_t(x)$ and diagonal by

$$r_t(x)=\sum_{y\ne x}R_t(x,y),\qquad R_t(x,x)=-r_t(x),\qquad R_t\mathbf1=0.$$

Here $\mathbf1$ is the column vector of ones. On an interval with bounded, continuous rates, the transition kernel $K_{t,t+h}$ satisfies

$$K_{t,t+h}(x,y)=\Pr(X_{t+h}=y\mid X_t=x)=\mathbf1_{\{x=y\}}+hR_t(x,y)+o(h).$$

The indicator $\mathbf1_A$ equals one when event $A$ holds and zero otherwise; $o(h)/h\to0$ as $h\downarrow0$. Rates have units of inverse time. A rate of two is legitimate: it does not mean a probability of two. The negative diagonal is also legitimate because it is a derivative of the probability of staying, rather than that probability itself. Off-diagonal nonnegativity and the zero row sum encode the only two possibilities in a sufficiently short interval: remain in the state or leave it.

Convention translation. The course writes $Q_t(y\mid x)$ for the rate from $x$ to $y$, with fixed source $x$ indexing a column. Its column-law equation is $p'_t=Q_tp_t$. This book uses $R_t=Q_t^{\mathsf T}$ and a row law, so $q'_t=q_tR_t$. The component equation is the safest reference. Campbell et al., PDF p3, also use source-row rates, although their initial clock runs from data toward noise. We use their rate interpretation without importing that time direction.

Take two states, zero and one, with rate two from zero to one and rate one in reverse. Then

$$R=\begin{pmatrix}-2&2\\1&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad q'_1=2q_0-q_1.$$

Starting from zero, the exact probability of state one is $q_t(1)=\tfrac23(1-e^{-3t})$. In particular, $q_1(1)=0.6334752878$. Accidentally computing $qR^{\mathsf T}$ at $q=(1,0)$ gives derivative $(-2,1)$, whose entries sum to minus one. It destroys probability immediately. The symmetric example in the lecture cannot expose this transpose because its generator equals its transpose; unequal rates make the convention observable.

6.2 Single-token jumps can create dependent sequences [12:29]

A network cannot generally output a row with $|\mathcal V|^L$ entries. The lecture restricts allowed jumps to neighboring sequences: two sequences are neighbors when they differ in one token. Write $x^{j\to v}$ for $x$ with position $j$ replaced by vocabulary symbol $v$. A sparse generator has $R_t(x,y)=0$ whenever more than one position differs. For input $x$, it therefore needs only the $L(|\mathcal V|-1)$ off-diagonal rates to its neighbors, plus the diagonal determined by their sum.

The network may output a rectangular array with one vocabulary vector per position. That storage layout is often called a factorized model in this literature. It expresses sparsity of the instantaneous transitions. It does not require that the rate at position $j$ depend only on $x_j$. A transformer can use the entire current sequence when predicting every rate. Nor does this layout imply that the joint probability mass function factors into independent token laws. Campbell et al., §4.2, PDF p5, make this distinction explicit: independent corruption of coordinates can yield dependent reverse dynamics when the original data are correlated.

For our running example, enlarge the vocabulary to $\mathcal V=\{M,0,1\}$, where $M$ means masked. Start at $MM$ and target $00$ or $11$. After the first token becomes zero, the remaining masked token should become zero with certainty; after the first becomes one, it should become one. Each jump changes one position, but its destination depends on what the other position currently says. This is enough to create perfect terminal correlation.

The distinction matters in implementation. If the current state is $0M$, a classifier receiving only the second token sees the same input as it would in $1M$. It cannot make both correct predictions. A classifier receiving the full sequence can distinguish them. Independence is therefore an architectural restriction one might impose, whereas single-token sparsity is a property of the generator we actually need. The latter preserves a large class of dependencies while making local simulation feasible.

There is a second distinction between a jump and a finite sampling step. An exact CTMC with bounded rates almost surely has no simultaneous jumps at exactly the same time. Over a positive interval, several jumps can occur in succession, and later rates can depend on earlier changes. A parallel update freezes that dependence while several token decisions are made. Its error is a numerical issue, even if the classifier supplied every instantaneous rate perfectly.

6.3 Exact events, uniformization, and finite steps [21:04]

The small-time formula suggests the Euler transition matrix $I+hR_t$, where $I$ is the identity matrix and $h>0$ is a time step. Its row sums equal one. Its off-diagonal entries are nonnegative. Its remaining requirement is the diagonal inequality

$$1-hr_t(x)\geq0\quad\text{for every }x,\qquad\text{equivalently}\quad h\max_x r_t(x)\leq1.$$

Thus conservation alone does not make an Euler step a probability distribution. For the asymmetric example, the maximum exit rate is two, so a step larger than one half fails. At $h=1$, the first row is $(-1,2)$. Clipping the negative entry and renormalizing constructs a different approximation; it does not make this Euler formula exact.

For a time-homogeneous generator $R$, an exact event sampler is simple. In state $x$ with $r(x)>0$, wait a random duration with exponential survival $\Pr(T>s)=e^{-r(x)s}$. Choose the next state $y\ne x$ with probability $R(x,y)/r(x)$, and repeat until the horizon. If $r(x)=0$, the state is absorbing. Exponential waiting times express the constant hazard of leaving the present state. For time-varying rates, the survival is instead $\exp[-\int r_s(x)\,ds]$ over the waiting interval. Drawing an exponential time using the rate at the interval's start silently freezes a changing hazard.

Proposition: homogeneous uniformization. Suppose $R$ is a finite-state generator and $\Lambda>0$ bounds all exit rates. Define $P=I+R/\Lambda$. Then $P$ is a stochastic matrix and the exact transition matrix is

$$e^{tR}=e^{-\Lambda t}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\Lambda t)^n}{n!}P^n.$$

Every entry of $P$ is nonnegative by the exit-rate bound, and every row sums to one. Since $R=\Lambda(P-I)$ and $P$ commutes with $I$, the exponential factors into $e^{-\Lambda t}e^{\Lambda tP}$. Expanding the latter exponential gives the displayed series. Each $P^n$ is stochastic; its Poisson weights are nonnegative and sum to one. Consequently the result is stochastic as well. Equivalently, run a Poisson clock of rate $\Lambda$ and perform a $P$ transition at every clock tick, allowing transitions that leave the state unchanged.

This supplies both an exact construction and a controlled numerical reference. Truncating the series leaves a Poisson tail; each entry's omitted contribution is bounded by that tail in exact arithmetic. Floating-point rounding adds its own error. In our program, uniformization at time one agrees with the two-state analytic probability to about $4\times10^{-16}$, while simulated holding times fluctuate at the expected Monte Carlo scale. A matrix exponential of the integrated rate matrix is generally insufficient for time-dependent generators: matrices at different times need not commute. The commuting special case discussed in Campbell et al., §4.1, PDF p5, avoids precisely that obstruction.

The lecture's scalable sampler uses independent per-position categorical Euler updates evaluated at the same frozen state. Each position must satisfy its own nonnegative stay-probability constraint. A product expansion shows that one-token changes have the intended order-$h$ probabilities, while two-token changes have order-$h^2$ probabilities when the rates are bounded. Gat et al., PDF p5, equation (20), give this expansion. This parallel kernel differs from the joint Euler kernel, which permits at most one changed position per step. It also differs from the Poisson tau-leaping method in Campbell et al.; both are approximations, with different finite-step definitions.

6.4 Conditional paths on tokens [37:41]

The recording next reconstructs the flow-matching recipe: choose a probability path, construct conditional dynamics, marginalize, and learn. We first design the path rather than asking a network to invent a transition matrix without a target. Let $Z\in\mathcal S$ be a clean data sequence drawn from the target mass function $p_{\mathrm{data}}$. Let $p_{0,j}$ be the initial token distribution at position $j$, with independent initial tokens. Choose a continuously differentiable, nondecreasing schedule $\kappa_t$ with $\kappa_0=0$, $\kappa_1=1$, and $\kappa_t<1$ for $t<1$. A dot denotes differentiation in time.

For one token and a fixed target symbol $z$, define the conditional probability mass function $p_t^z$ by

$$p_t^z(x)=(1-\kappa_t)p_0(x)+\kappa_t\delta_z(x),\qquad\delta_z(x)=\mathbf1_{\{x=z\}}.$$

This is a mixture of distributions. Sampling it means choosing a noise symbol with probability $1-\kappa_t$, or the terminal symbol with probability $\kappa_t$. Tokens are labels: the formula does not average their numerical identifiers or embed a partially formed token between them. The initial law is independent of the chosen endpoint, and the terminal law concentrates on it. For multiple tokens, use independent corruption conditional on the entire fixed target sequence:

$$p_t^z(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{L}\left[(1-\kappa_t)p_{0,j}(x_j)+\kappa_t\delta_{z_j}(x_j)\right].$$

The product concerns the noisy coordinates given $Z=z$. Marginalizing over correlated targets generally destroys that product structure. In the masked example $p_{0,j}=\delta_M$, choose $\kappa_t=t$. Each target token is revealed with probability $t$, independently given the clean pair. Averaging over $Z=00$ and $Z=11$ gives

$$\begin{aligned}q_t(MM)&=(1-t)^2,\\q_t(0M)=q_t(M0)=q_t(1M)=q_t(M1)&=\frac{t(1-t)}2,\\q_t(00)=q_t(11)&=\frac{t^2}2,\\q_t(01)=q_t(10)&=0.\end{aligned}$$

At time one half, the all-masked state has probability one quarter and each of the other six reachable states has probability one eighth. These seven entries sum to one. Invalid pairs remain absent throughout the path, even though each coordinate is independently corrupted conditional on the target. This gives a complete probability law against which a sampler can be checked; checking only the endpoint or only the two marginal token frequencies would discard useful evidence.

Gat et al., §2.3, PDF p3, equations (7)–(10), formulate a broader family with endpoint couplings and mixtures of several conditional distributions. The course's independent-noise mixture is the specialization used here. Choosing a richer schedule changes how training examples distribute information across time. It does not remove the need to specify a generator that actually produces the proposed path.

6.5 From conditional rates to posterior-averaged rates [44:27]

Define the interior hazard $\lambda_t=\dot\kappa_t/(1-\kappa_t)$. For one token, consider the conditional generator

$$R_t^z(x,y)=\lambda_t\big[\delta_z(y)-\delta_x(y)\big].$$

When $x\ne z$, the only positive destination is $z$, with rate $\lambda_t$, and the diagonal is $-\lambda_t$. When $x=z$, the row is zero. Thus a token that has already reached its known conditional target stays there. The row sums vanish and off-diagonal entries are nonnegative. This is a candidate conditional mechanism; the next section will verify it by the forward equation.

For a sequence, sum the position generators. A transition from $x$ to $x^{j\to v}$, where $v\ne x_j$, has rate $\lambda_t\delta_{z_j}(v)$, and the joint diagonal is minus the sum of all position exit rates. Position-specific diagonal contributions must be added, because there is only one joint self-transition. Keeping several token diagonals as if they were distinct joint destinations would duplicate the same state.

At generation time we do not know $z$. The clean sequence is latent, and its relevance depends on the noisy sequence currently visible. Define the marginal law and posterior on states where $q_t(x)>0$:

$$q_t(x)=\sum_z p_t^z(x)p_{\mathrm{data}}(z),\qquad \pi_t(z\mid x)=\frac{p_t^z(x)p_{\mathrm{data}}(z)}{q_t(x)}.$$

The marginal generator averages conditional rates under this posterior:

$$R_t(x,y)=\sum_z\pi_t(z\mid x)R_t^z(x,y).$$

This is the discrete marginalization trick of the course notes, Theorem 36, and Gat et al., Theorem 2, PDF p5. Posterior weights are essential. At $0M$, the endpoint $11$ has posterior probability zero, although its prior probability is one half. Averaging with the prior would let the second token become one, creating an impossible sentence. Conditioning on the present sequence removes that destination.

For a one-token replacement, write $\rho_{t,j}(v\mid x)=\Pr(Z_j=v\mid X_t=x)$. Posterior averaging gives the especially useful form

$$R_t(x,x^{j\to v})=\lambda_t\rho_{t,j}(v\mid x)\quad(v\ne x_j),\qquad R_t(x,x)=-\lambda_t\sum_{j=1}^{L}\left[1-\rho_{t,j}(x_j\mid x)\right].$$

Only token marginals of the posterior are required to construct these single-token rates. They still condition on the entire noisy sequence. The collection of token marginals does not specify a factorized joint posterior over clean sequences; no such factorization is needed. In the running example, the two clean tokens are perfectly correlated under the posterior at $MM$, even though their separate probabilities each equal one half.

Zero-mass states need an explicit convention. The posterior formula says nothing at $01$ or $10$, because they never occur under the designed path. A classifier trained only on that path cannot identify its outputs there. In the finite example we extend those rows by zero, making invalid completed sequences absorbing. This is an extension used to define approximate samplers, not a claim that their posterior was learned. More generally, any extension must retain the generator constraints and the regularity required by the existence argument; its consequences matter if numerical or model error sends the sampler outside the ideal support.

6.6 Gain, loss, and the marginalization proof [49:32]

The discrete counterpart of the continuity equation counts inflow and outflow. Condition on the current state $i$, multiply its short-time transition probabilities by $q_t(i)$, and sum. Because the state space is finite, the derivative passes through the sum. For destination $j$ this gives

$$\begin{aligned}q'_t(j)&=\sum_iq_t(i)R_t(i,j)\\&=\underbrace{\sum_{i\ne j}q_t(i)R_t(i,j)}_{\text{incoming probability per unit time}}-\underbrace{q_t(j)r_t(j)}_{\text{outgoing probability per unit time}}.\end{aligned}$$

In row notation, $q'_t=q_tR_t$. Total probability is preserved because $q'_t\mathbf1=q_tR_t\mathbf1=0$. Conservation by itself does not prove nonnegativity; that also depends on nonnegative off-diagonal rates. The diagonal removes only probability already at the state, while every incoming contribution is nonnegative.

Proposition: finite-state forward characterization. On $[0,\tau]$, suppose $R_t$ is continuous and bounded and obeys the generator constraints. Given an initial probability row $q_0$, there is a unique CTMC transition law with these rates. A continuously differentiable probability row $q_t$ is its marginal law exactly when $q'_t=q_tR_t$ and its initial value is $q_0$.

For existence, approximate the continuous generator uniformly by piecewise constant generators on refining partitions. Each interval's transition matrix is stochastic by homogeneous uniformization, so products of these matrices are stochastic. Finite-dimensional linear-ODE stability gives convergence to the solution of $U'_{s,t}=U_{s,t}R_t$, $U_{s,s}=I$. One can see this stability directly by subtracting the two integral equations: uniform coefficient error plus Grönwall's inequality bounds their difference by a constant times the coefficient error. Entrywise limits preserve nonnegativity and row sums. Uniqueness of the linear ODE gives $U_{s,t}U_{t,v}=U_{s,v}$, the transition composition rule. These kernels define the finite-state Markov law; bounded exit rates permit a dominating Poisson clock, so only finitely many candidate events occur on the interval. Necessity of the forward equation follows from the short-time calculation above. Conversely, the actual marginal row and any proposed row satisfying that ODE with the same initial condition coincide by linear-ODE uniqueness.

This supplies the missing sufficiency step in a path construction. Verifying an equation with the wrong initial condition would identify a different process. The result corresponds to the course's Theorem 33 and appendix C; we give the stochastic-product construction to make positivity explicit.

First verify the one-token conditional formula. Multiplying the conditional row by its generator gives

$$\begin{aligned}(p_t^zR_t^z)(y)&=\lambda_t\left[\delta_z(y)\sum_xp_t^z(x)-p_t^z(y)\right]\\&=\lambda_t[\delta_z(y)-p_t^z(y)]\\&=\dot\kappa_t[\delta_z(y)-p_0(y)]=\partial_tp_t^z(y).\end{aligned}$$

The normalization of $p_t^z$ is used in the first simplification. For multiple tokens, differentiating the product path gives a sum over positions. In the term for position $j$, all other factors remain unchanged and the one-token calculation acts on its factor. This is exactly multiplication by the sum of the single-position generators. Thus the conditional joint path satisfies its forward equation, without assuming anything about independence after marginalization.

Proposition: posterior marginalization. Suppose the conditional probability rows satisfy $(p_t^z)'=p_t^zR_t^z$ on an interval, and the posterior-averaged generator has a continuous bounded extension to all states there. Then it generates their mixture $q_t$ from its mixture initial law.

A posterior average preserves each nonnegative off-diagonal entry and every zero row sum. For each destination $y$, finite summation gives

$$\begin{aligned}q'_t(y)&=\sum_zp_{\mathrm{data}}(z)\sum_xp_t^z(x)R_t^z(x,y)\\&=\sum_{x:q_t(x)>0}q_t(x)\sum_z\pi_t(z\mid x)R_t^z(x,y)\\&=\sum_xq_t(x)R_t(x,y).\end{aligned}$$

When $q_t(x)=0$, every nonnegative term $p_t^z(x)p_{\mathrm{data}}(z)$ is zero, so the omitted contribution in the first sum is zero; the extended row also contributes zero after multiplication by $q_t(x)$. The forward characterization now proves the claim with the specified initial law. The proof identifies an averaged generator, not a unique generator among all those that could follow the same path.

For example, at $MM$ the masked-pair chain has total exit rate $2/(1-t)$. Its outgoing probability flux is $q_t(MM)2/(1-t)=2(1-t)$, agreeing with $-q'_t(MM)$. At $00$, incoming contributions from $0M$ and $M0$ are each $t/2$, so $q'_t(00)=t$, exactly the derivative of $t^2/2$. The code checks all nine component equations at $t=0.37$, including the two zero-mass states.

6.7 An exploding hazard can have a finite terminal limit [57:55]

For $\kappa_t=t$, $\lambda_t=1/(1-t)$ diverges as $t\uparrow1$. The bounded-rate theorem applies on every compact interval ending before one. It cannot simply be invoked on the closed interval by ignoring the final denominator. Nevertheless, this particular conditional process has an exact terminal construction. Given a token currently different from its known target at time $s$, its probability of surviving without a jump until time $t$ is

$$S(t\mid s)=\exp\left[-\int_s^t\frac{\dot\kappa_r}{1-\kappa_r}\,dr\right]=\frac{1-\kappa_t}{1-\kappa_s},\qquad s\leq t<1.$$

For the linear schedule, the jump time conditional on still waiting at $s$ is uniform on $(s,1)$. The probability of never jumping before one is zero. Once it jumps to its target, its exit rate becomes zero. Thus the conditional token makes at most one actual jump, despite its divergent pre-jump hazard. An infinite integrated hazard makes eventual completion certain; it does not itself count infinitely many events.

Our masked-pair process also has an exact marginal construction. Draw two independent uniform reveal times, one per position. At the first reveal, choose zero or one fairly. At the second, copy the already revealed bit. Ties have probability zero. Each token is still masked at time $t$ with probability $1-t$, and the shared chosen bit has the target distribution, so this construction has exactly the seven-state path above. It takes two jumps and reaches a valid terminal sentence almost surely. Sampling the shared bit before the clocks instead gives the same law; the sequential description emphasizes posterior updating.

This argument uses the mask path's special support. With exact posteriors, a revealed non-mask token must equal its clean token, so its rate of changing is zero. For a general uniform-noise path, a visible token can be corrupted noise, and a marginal process may revise it. One cannot transfer the conditional one-jump statement to every learned marginal chain. Approximate classifiers can also assign nonzero mass to changing an already revealed token. Freezing those tokens is a model constraint that should be stated rather than attributed automatically to approximate posterior inference.

A finite-step implementation faces another boundary. With $N$ uniform steps, $h=1/N$ and $t_k=k/N$, a masked position's reveal probability is $h/(1-t_k)=1/(N-k)$. This remains at most one and equals one on the last step. However, at $MM$ the joint Euler condition would require $2h/(1-t_k)\leq1$, which fails at the last step. The per-position product is a valid categorical kernel there, but it is not the joint Euler kernel. Its validity does not certify its accuracy near the singular endpoint. The numerical experiment below evaluates that error directly instead of extending an interior order estimate without checking its constants.

6.8 Learning rates by terminal-token classification [1:04:10]

The rate formula contains one unknown object: $\rho_{t,j}(v\mid x)$, the posterior probability of a clean token given the noisy full sequence. Let a model with parameters $\theta$ output a categorical probability vector $f_{\theta,j}(\cdot\mid t,x)$ for each position. The rate model substitutes $f_{\theta,j}$ for $\rho_{t,j}$, multiplies the off-diagonal entries by $\lambda_t$, and constructs the diagonal by conservation. A softmax output can enforce probability normalization, while the nonnegative schedule enforces nonnegative jump rates.

Training requires no simulated trajectory. Draw a clean sequence $Z\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}$, draw a time $T$ uniformly on $[0,1]$, and sample a corrupted state $X\sim p_T^Z$. The capital $T$ here denotes a randomly sampled training time. Minimize the token-summed negative log likelihood

$$\mathcal L(\theta)=\mathbb E\left[-\sum_{j=1}^{L}\log f_{\theta,j}(Z_j\mid T,X)\right].$$

The joint sampling procedure automatically creates examples from the desired posterior classification problem; we do not have to compute posterior labels numerically. The path, clean example, and corrupted sequence are sufficient. The course's Algorithm 8 gives this recipe. Gat et al., PDF p7, equation (28) and Proposition 5, establish the corresponding posterior minimization for their broader mixture construction.

Proposition: categorical population optimum. At a time and state with positive probability, let $r(v)=\rho_{t,j}(v\mid x)$ be the true token posterior. Over all categorical probability vectors $f$, the conditional cross-entropy is minimized by $f=r$. The conclusion holds almost everywhere under the training distribution, not at contexts of zero training probability.

Define entropy $H(r)=-\sum_{v:r(v)>0}r(v)\log r(v)$. If $f(v)=0$ where $r(v)>0$, the cross-entropy is infinite. Otherwise,

$$-\sum_vr(v)\log f(v)=H(r)+\underbrace{\sum_{v:r(v)>0}r(v)\log\frac{r(v)}{f(v)}}_{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(r\Vert f)}.$$

The convention $0\log0=0$ removes zero-posterior terms. Applying $-\log u\geq1-u$ to $u=f(v)/r(v)$ gives $D_{\mathrm{KL}}\geq1-\sum_{v:r(v)>0}f(v)\geq0$. Equality requires $f(v)=r(v)$ on the posterior support. Normalization then leaves no mass outside that support, proving equality of the complete vectors. Averaging this decomposition over training contexts and positions yields a nonnegative excess risk, with the stated almost-everywhere qualification.

This is a population result over the full probability simplex. A finite softmax vector has strictly positive entries, so a posterior with exact zeros may be approached only as logits diverge; a finite parameter minimizer need not attain it. A restricted neural family may also fail to represent the posterior, finite data can misestimate it, and optimization can stop short. None of those effects is a contradiction of the proposition. Nor does a small empirical cross-entropy automatically bound endpoint generation error under an arbitrary singular schedule and numerical solver.

For the two-token case, an unrestricted tabular classifier is enough to perform real fitting transparently. At $MM$, both clean-token posterior probabilities of zero are one half; at every other reachable state, the observed bit determines both clean tokens. With uniform training time, $\Pr(X=MM)=\int_0^1(1-t)^2dt=1/3$. The irreducible population token-summed loss is therefore $\tfrac23\log2=0.4620981204$. Positive loss at the optimum records genuine ambiguity at $MM$, rather than model failure.

6.9 A complete finite-state experiment [1:07:51]

The companion standard-library program implements the asymmetric chain, uniformization, event-time reveal sampling, tabular posterior fitting, and exact enumeration of parallel sampling kernels. Run python code/ch06.py --write-figure from the book directory. The recorded results file includes seeds, counts, distributions, residuals, and elapsed time. These are original finite-state teaching experiments, separate from the language-model demonstrations in the recording. No neural language model is trained or benchmarked.

First isolate numerical sampling error with the true posterior. If both tokens reveal in the same parallel step while the state is $MM$, each independently chooses a fair bit from its marginal. They disagree with probability one half. If the tokens reveal in different steps, the first reveal determines the second and they agree. Under the linear schedule each token's reveal-step index is uniform on the $N$ steps, independently of the other's index: survival to step $k$ is $(N-k)/N$, multiplied by reveal probability $1/(N-k)$, giving $1/N$. Therefore

$$\Pr(\text{invalid terminal pair})=\frac12\Pr(\text{same reveal step})=\frac{1}{2N}.$$

The two valid pairs each have probability $\tfrac12-\tfrac1{4N}$ and the two invalid pairs each have probability $1/(4N)$. The total variation distance, defined for probability mass functions $a,b$ by $\tfrac12\sum_x|a(x)-b(x)|$, is consequently $1/(2N)$. Both individual terminal token marginals are exactly fair at every $N$. This is a concrete failure of marginal-only evaluation. Exact enumeration confirms invalid masses $0.5$, $0.125$, $0.03125$, and $0.0078125$ for $N=1,4,16,64$ respectively, without Monte Carlo uncertainty.

The following standalone snippet checks conservation and the closed-form asymmetric probability. It deliberately uses unequal rates. Changing the multiplication to a transpose makes the mass-derivative check fail.

import math

rates = [[-2.0, 2.0], [1.0, -1.0]]
q = [1.0, 0.0]
derivative = [sum(q[i] * rates[i][j] for i in range(2))
              for j in range(2)]
assert abs(sum(derivative)) < 1e-12
probability = 2 * (1 - math.exp(-3)) / 3
print(f"derivative={derivative}, P(X_1=1)={probability:.10f}")

The full run uses base seed $61842026$. For $100{,}000$ holding-time trajectories, it estimates the asymmetric terminal probability as $0.63236$, against $0.63347529$ analytically; the binomial standard error is $0.00152376$. The average trajectory has $1.54884$ jumps. A separate $100{,}000$-sample event-time check of the masked path at time one half has empirical total variation $0.00329$ from its exact seven-state law and no invalid pair. That empirical discrepancy measures sampling fluctuation; the exact event construction itself has no time-step error.

Next fit the classifier from $80{,}000$ independently sampled clean/corrupted examples, using seed $61842027$. A count-based maximum-likelihood table observes $26{,}731$ all-masked examples, of which $13{,}462$ have target zero. It estimates the ambiguous posterior as $\widehat p=0.50361004$. Every partially revealed context has a deterministic observed label in this synthetic distribution. Pooling across times is justified here because these posterior probabilities happen to be time-independent; it would be a representation restriction in a problem where they vary with time.

A held-out set of $100{,}000$ examples, seed $61842028$, gives token-summed negative log likelihood $0.46446738$ with estimated standard error $0.00206939$. Its population loss can also be computed exactly as $-[\log\widehat p+\log(1-\widehat p)]/3=0.46211550$. The excess over the oracle population loss is only $0.00001738$. The larger held-out difference is within ordinary sampling fluctuation, so it must not be labeled an exact generalization gap. Under exact sequential reveal sampling, this fitted posterior gives terminal probability $\widehat p$ to $00$ and $1-\widehat p$ to $11$, hence terminal total variation $|\widehat p-1/2|=0.00361004$.

The experiment separates three effects. The table can represent the true posterior, so there is no representation error within this finite construction. Sample counts cause finite-fit error even with exact event sampling. Frozen parallel updates cause a distinct solver error even with the oracle classifier. The displayed solver curve holds the classifier exact; the finite-fit terminal calculation holds the event sampler exact. The program does not assert that these errors add linearly for a general learned model. Its recorded run takes well under a second on the local CPU, and the recorded result is a measurement of this tiny experiment alone.

6.10 What generalizes, and what remains a systems question [1:15:41]

The lecture closes by comparing discrete diffusion with autoregressive generation. A sparse CTMC allows dependencies to be resolved in an order that changes with the partially generated sequence. Parallel numerical updates can reveal several tokens per model call. Those are mechanisms, not a universal speed theorem. Cost also depends on the number of denoiser calls, the cost of each full-sequence pass, cache reuse, hardware utilization, sequence length, and the quality criterion. Autoregressive decoding has a particularly direct fixed-prefix cache pattern. The recording's questions about speed and editing should remain questions until a specified implementation and comparison measure them.

Similarly, a token representation's natural order and its generation order are different concepts. Text and biological sequences have meaningful ordered positions; a model need not generate those positions in their physical or written order. An editing procedure that freezes selected observations needs a compatible conditional distribution and corruption/training scheme. Arbitrarily freezing a subset during an unconditional sampler does not by itself prove exact sampling from the data distribution conditioned on that subset. In our example, conditioning one observed bit to zero forces the other to zero; the posterior calculation verifies this special case completely.

The final research connection is to generators. For any real-valued function $f$ on the finite state space, define its generator action by

$$[\mathcal L_tf](x)=\sum_{y\ne x}R_t(x,y)[f(y)-f(x)].$$

Conditioning one short step shows that this equals the instantaneous change of the expected value of $f$ given current state $x$. Multiplying by $q_t(x)$ and summing gives $\tfrac{d}{dt}\mathbb E[f(X_t)]=\mathbb E[\mathcal L_tf(X_t)]$. Choosing state-indicator functions recovers each component of the forward equation. For continuous deterministic trajectories with velocity $u_t$, the corresponding action is $u_t(x)\cdot\nabla f(x)$; for the earlier Itô models with drift $b_t$ and scalar Brownian amplitude $\sigma_t$, it is $b_t(x)\cdot\nabla f(x)+\tfrac12\sigma_t^2\Delta f(x)$ on suitable smooth test functions. These expressions describe different processes through the same evolution-of-observables viewpoint.

Holderrieth et al., Generator Matching, ICLR 2025, PDF pp4–6, equation (8), equation (11), and Proposition 1, formalize this viewpoint and posterior-average conditional generators. Here the finite-state derivation already supplies a complete example: linearity of the forward equation makes posterior marginalization work. Extending it to other state spaces requires appropriate test-function domains and well-posedness assumptions; the finite matrix proof is not automatically a proof for every infinite-dimensional operator.

The useful design freedom is consequently precise. One can choose paths, conditional dynamics, and numerical samplers, while checking that they fit together. A posterior classifier supplies valid rates after normalization, but only the ideal posterior and a justified sampling limit recover the chosen law. The two-token construction demonstrates why the validation target must include correlations, support, and solver behavior as well as the training loss.

6.11 Problems with worked solutions

Problem 1 — Diagnose an orientation error

A three-state chain has source-row generator $R=\begin{pmatrix}-3&2&1\\4&-4&0\\0&5&-5\end{pmatrix}$. Starting at state one (the first row), find the probability derivative and the largest Euler step valid for every initial state. Explain how a transpose changes the conservation test.

Worked solution

The initial probability row is $(1,0,0)$, so $qR=(-3,2,1)$; its sum is zero. Exit rates are $3,4,5$, hence every Euler row is nonnegative exactly when $h\leq1/5$. The mistaken row $qR^{\mathsf T}=(-3,4,0)$ sums to one and immediately increases total mass. Transposing a matrix is legitimate only together with transposing the probability-vector convention and changing the multiplication order.

Problem 2 — A conditional hazard with a different schedule

Use $\kappa_t=t^2$ for one initially masked token with a known non-mask target. Derive its jump rate, its survival from time zero, and an exact jump-time sampler. Does the divergent endpoint rate imply infinitely many actual jumps?

Worked solution

The hazard is $\lambda_t=2t/(1-t^2)$ on $0\leq t<1$. Integrating gives survival $1-t^2$, so the jump-time cumulative distribution is $t^2$. If $U$ is uniform on $(0,1)$, choose jump time $\sqrt U$. The token jumps once to the absorbing target. The hazard diverges near one, but the state-dependent exit rate becomes zero after that single event. The bounded theorem applies on every $[0,\tau]$ with $\tau<1$; the explicit construction supplies the terminal extension.

Problem 3 — Show dependence without dense jumps

For the masked-pair example at $t=1/2$, calculate $\Pr((X_{1/2})_1=0)$ and $\Pr((X_{1/2})_2=0)$, where the subscripts inside parentheses index positions. Compare their product with the probability of current state $00$. Then calculate the rates from $0M$ to $00$ and $01$.

Worked solution

Each position is zero with probability $t/2=1/4$. Their product is $1/16$, whereas the current joint probability of $00$ is $t^2/2=1/8$. The current coordinates are dependent. At $0M$, the posterior clean sequence is certainly $00$. Since $\lambda_{1/2}=2$, the rate to $00$ is two and the rate to $01$ is zero. Only one position changes in either candidate transition; it is the posterior's dependence on the other position that selects the correct destination.

Problem 4 — Cross-entropy identifies a posterior only where observed

A context has true categorical posterior $(3/4,1/4,0)$. Compare predictions $(1/2,1/2,0)$ and $(3/4,1/4,0)$ using excess cross-entropy. What changes if the model assigns probability zero to the first symbol, or if the context itself has zero training probability?

Worked solution

The fair prediction's excess is $\tfrac34\log(3/2)+\tfrac14\log(1/2)\approx0.130812$ nats. Predicting the true vector gives zero excess. A zero prediction for the first symbol has infinite cross-entropy because that label has positive true probability. If the entire context has zero training probability, its contribution to the population risk is zero whatever its prediction; the posterior is not identified there. Finally, a finite ordinary softmax cannot output the third probability as exactly zero, so equality may be an infimum rather than an attained finite-parameter optimum.

Problem 5 — Separate fitting error from parallel sampling error

Replace the fair posterior at $MM$ by a fitted probability $p\in(0,1)$ of zero; keep the deterministic posteriors after a reveal. Derive the invalid terminal probability under $N$ frozen parallel steps. Give the exact terminal distribution under event sampling, and explain why these two error measurements answer different questions.

Worked solution

Reveal-step indices remain independent uniform indices. If they coincide, independent bit choices disagree with probability $2p(1-p)$; otherwise the later token copies the first. Thus invalid mass is $2p(1-p)/N$. Exact event sampling uses a single bit draw at the first reveal, so it produces probabilities $p$ and $1-p$ on $00$ and $11$ and no invalid pair. Its total variation from the balanced target is $|p-1/2|$, measuring fitting error with exact dynamics. The invalid mass of the parallel sampler measures one aspect of solver error; for $p\ne1/2$ it is not the complete distance from the balanced target. Both class imbalance and invalid support must be evaluated.

Problem 6 — Design a defensible generation comparison

A proposal claims that a discrete denoiser is faster because it updates all positions in parallel, and validates its samples only by token frequencies. Design a minimal comparison that could support a narrower speed claim and detect the two-token failure. Specify what the mathematical derivations alone can certify.

Worked solution

Fix target sequence lengths, hardware, batch sizes, quality criteria, and complete generation workloads. Measure wall time and resource use at several denoiser step counts, alongside an autoregressive baseline with its available cache mechanism. Include repeated runs and a quality-versus-cost curve, because fewer steps may damage the joint law. On the synthetic pair task, report the four terminal pair frequencies, invalid mass, and total variation; token frequencies alone accept the one-step distribution with half its mass on invalid pairs. Distinguish exact oracle sampling from fitted classifiers so the comparison locates the error source. The derivations certify valid instantaneous rates and the ideal probability path under their assumptions. They do not certify hardware speed, optimized implementation quality, or finite-step language-model quality.
